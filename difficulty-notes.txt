====== Difficulty Curves (davetech.co.uk) Applied to Crossfire ======

[[https://www.davetech.co.uk/difficultycurves|The article]] is written for linear single-player platformers, so some of it maps onto Crossfire directly and some of it fights Crossfire's basic shape. Here is the split.

===== What applies =====

**The "difficulty saw" instead of a curve.** This is the article's core idea and it applies cleanly at the //map-set// level, not the game level. Crossfire has no global curve — it is an open world where a level 3 character can walk out of Scorn and into something that kills them instantly. But every individual dungeon, tower, or quest chain is a small linear experience, and those absolutely should dip when they introduce their gimmick and then rise. Most Crossfire dungeons open at full intensity on room one.

**The definition of "new mechanic" is where Crossfire is worst hit.** The article counts new enemy types, new abilities, new item rules, and old mechanics used in novel ways. Crossfire's mechanic surface is enormous — skills, praying and god-granted powers, spell paths, resistances and immunities, item power limits, alchemy, marking runes, dragon players, weapon improvement — and the game teaches almost none of it in-game. The de facto teaching modality is the wiki and asking on IRC. That is exactly the failure the article describes. Expanded below.

**"It is impossible to make a game for everyone."** Crossfire has implicitly targeted one archetype for 30 years: the player who reads external documentation and enjoys opaque systems. That is a legitimate choice, but it should be a //decision//, not an accident, and it should be stated so map authors design toward it consistently. Expanded below.

**Don't punish failure with increased difficulty.** Crossfire's death penalty is exp loss plus stat depletion — the player who just failed is now measurably weaker facing the same content. This is the article's explicit "don't." It reads like an engine problem, but most of it is not - the numbers are already tunable in ''lib/config/settings'', and the depletion is curable by any player who knows how. Expanded below.

**Don't hide mechanics as puzzle solutions.** Plenty of Crossfire maps require knowing something never taught anywhere in the game to progress. Expanded below.

**Adrenaline pacing and backward callbacks.** Both apply to dungeon layout and to the quest system. A quest chain that revisits an earlier obstacle with the player's new tools is well within what staged ''.quests'' definitions already support. Expanded below.

**Monitoring.** Death-location tracking and completion drop-off are the article's most actionable ideas and the ones Crossfire has never done systematically.

===== The mechanic surface, expanded =====

Two different things get called "too hard" and only one of them is a difficulty problem. A player who dies to a monster that hits harder than they can heal has learned something and can act on it next time. A player whose fireball does nothing to a fire elemental, whose magic bullet bounces off a level 6 gnome, or who cannot wear the artifact they just spent an hour earning has learned nothing at all — the game told them nothing, so they alt-tab to the wiki or they stop playing. That is a teaching failure, not a tuning failure, and no amount of difficulty rebalancing fixes it.

Split the surface three ways, because who can fix it differs for each.

==== 1. Local mechanics - the map that uses it should teach it ====

These are specific to the content in front of the player, so the map introducing the problem is the map that owes them the explanation.

**Resistances and immunities.** ''fire_elemental'' carries ''resist_fire 100''. A pyromancer can empty their entire mana pool into one and nothing happens, with no message distinguishing "immune" from "you missed."

**Spell reflection.** ''gnome'' is level 6 with ''reflect_spell 1'', which means it is content a brand new spellcaster meets, and their own bolt comes back at them with no explanation offered. Angels and nazgul do the same thing at the other end of the level range.

**Attacktype gating.** Monsters that only take damage from magic or blessed weapons, undead that need the right attacktype, ''slaying'' matches that make one weapon in the player's pack five times better than the one they are holding.

**Terrain and timed obstacles.** Gates on a cycle, teleporters, moving walls, spinners — anything where the correct play is to stand still and watch for ten seconds, which is the opposite of what a player under pressure does.

**Quest-item handling.** ''check_inv'' gates keyed on ''slaying'', marking runes, and NPC ''@match'' keywords that cannot be guessed from anything visible in the map.

==== 2. Character-wide mechanics - start areas, temples, and guild NPCs ====

These are not specific to any one dungeon, so teaching them in every dungeon is noise. They belong once, in a place the player passes through early, and again at the point of first contact.

**Skills and literacy** (you need the skill object before the action works, and books silently refuse readers without literacy), **praying and gods** (converting, grace regeneration, what a god actually grants), **spell paths** (attuned, repelled, and denied, including paths your own god denies), **item power** (the level-relative cap on worn equipment), **curses and damnation**, **alchemy and weapon improvement** (both are multi-step sequences with irreversible failure states), and **encumbrance** (armour weight costing you speed and spell point regeneration).

==== 3. Engine-side - not a map author's problem ====

The wording of refusal messages and the general absence of a "that did nothing, and here is why" feedback line. Worth listing separately so nobody tries to paper over them with signs. The death penalty looks like it belongs here too, but most of it does not - see the death penalty section below.

==== What the engine already hands a map author ====

The modalities are all there, but they are not equal, and two of the ones commonly cited do not fire on their own.

**''arch magic_mouth''** — type 98, ''invisible 1'', ''move_on walk fly_low'', and it honours ''connected'', so a lever or pressure plate can fire it. Un-missable, leaves nothing on the floor, and the lesson can be timed to the moment it matters. This is the best teaching object in the game and it is mostly used for jokes.

**''arch rune_mark''** — the same type and the same walk trigger, but visible. Use it when the player should see that something was deliberately left here. ''scorn/misc/beginners2'' uses one exactly right: "If you enter this corridor and don't react fast, you will die!"

**''arch sign''** — needs an ''apply'', so it can be walked past, but it has no skill requirement and no level check, which makes it the safe choice for anything a level 1 character must not miss. Set ''food'' to limit the number of reads (unset means unlimited).

**Books** — portable and re-readable, but ''types/book/book.cpp'' requires the skill named in the book's ''skill'' field and compares the book's ''level'' against the reader's skill level plus 5. A new player without literacy gets "You are unable to decipher the strange symbols" and walks away with nothing. NOTE: if you teach with a book, set ''level 0'', and never put a required lesson in one.

**NPC ''@match'' dialogue** — the richest option and the only one that rewards a player for asking the right question. ''scorn/misc/beginners_entry'' is the model: several synonyms per ''@match'', ''@question'' and ''@reply'' lines advertising the follow-ups, and a ''@match *'' fallback. The failure mode is the player who never types anything, so dialogue supplements a lesson, it does not carry one.

**Map ''msg'' and region ''msg''** — correcting the list further down: neither displays automatically. ''server/c_misc.cpp'' prints the map's ''msg'' only from ''mapinfo'' and the region's only from ''whereami''. They are flavour for players who already poke at commands, not a teaching channel for someone who does not know the commands exist.

**''knowledge_marker''** — the one that should be everywhere and is not. Put ''knowledge_marker monster:fire_elemental'' on a sign, rune, or book and reading it permanently adds that monster to the player's ''knowledge'' list, complete with the description the server generates from the archetype (attacktypes, resistances, abilities). Valid prefixes are ''monster:'', ''god:'', ''alchemy:'', and ''message:'', multiple entries separated by ''/''. Grep the entire map set and three files use it — ''start/newbieshouse'', ''quests/peterm/FireTemple/FourthFloor'', and one python command. That is the largest piece of unused teaching machinery in the game, and it is the only one whose lesson survives the player logging out.

==== Worked examples ====

All map-side, no engine work, no new archetypes.

**1. The immunity that eats your only attack.** A fire dungeon full of ''fire_elemental''. Put a burned-out camp on the approach square with an invisible mouth over it, so the player is told before they commit mana rather than after.

<code>
arch magic_mouth
msg
Three bodies, and the scorching runs outward from them, not inward.
Whatever finished them here, it was not the fire they were throwing.
endmsg
knowledge_marker monster:fire_elemental
x 5
y 3
end
</code>

The ''msg'' handles the immediate decision. The ''knowledge_marker'' handles the next character the player rolls, and the one after that.

**2. Demonstrate the gimmick, do not describe it.** For a spell-reflection map, room one gets a single ''gnome'' in an alcove the player can shoot into but not walk into, with a retreat corridor behind them and a rune on the approach tile. The player fires one bolt, watches it come back, takes a survivable amount of damage, and now understands ''reflect_spell'' permanently. A sign saying "these gnomes reflect spells" teaches the same fact and none of the reflex.

<code>
arch rune_mark
msg
Scratched into the floor: "THEY THROW IT BACK - ASK ME HOW I KNOW"
endmsg
knowledge_marker monster:gnome
x 3
y 8
end
</code>

**3. Fire the lesson at the moment it matters.** For a timed gate or a paralysis room, do not put the explanation at the map entrance where it will be forgotten three rooms later. Put a ''magic_mouth'' with ''connected'' on the same trigger as the gate, so the first cycle of the gate is narrated while the player is standing there watching it.

<code>
arch magic_mouth
connected 12
msg
The gate slams, waits, and grinds open again. Whatever runs it, it is
counting, not watching.
endmsg
x 9
y 4
end
</code>

**4. The artifact you cannot wear.** Item power is a level-relative cap, and the refusal message is engine-side, but the treasure room is not. Put a sign on the pedestal explaining the rule in-world, and put a lower-power alternative on the same square so the trip still pays out for the character who found it early.

<code>
arch sign
name inscription on the pedestal
msg
"Ward and weight alike answer to the wearer. Bear more than your name
can carry and it will refuse your hand until you have grown into it."
Below, in a later hand: the greaves beside it are plainer, and will serve.
endmsg
x 12
y 6
end
</code>

**5. Let the player ask before they commit.** Spell paths and god grants are the worst version of this problem, because the cost of finding out is a conversion or a wasted spellbook. A temple NPC with ''@match'' on the god's name, on "path", and on "denied", answering with what the god grants and what it locks out, turns an hour-long mistake into a thirty-second conversation. Hang ''knowledge_marker god:<name>'' off the temple's founding-inscription sign while you are in there, so the answer stays in the player's ''knowledge'' list.

**6. Call the mechanic back later.** This is the article's backward-callback point applied to teaching rather than to layout. If room one taught spell reflection with one caged gnome, the map's late-game room should put reflectors behind a line of ordinary casters, so the player has to re-derive the lesson under pressure. A mechanic taught once and never reused reads as a gimmick; taught once and re-tested twice, it reads as the dungeon having a subject.

==== Conventions worth writing into Info/mapguide ====

  - Any map whose intended solution depends on a mechanic not taught in the start areas must carry an in-map teaching object, and that object goes before the first place the mechanic can kill you.
  - Where failure is lethal, use ''magic_mouth'' or ''rune_mark'', not a sign. Signs are for lessons the player can afford to walk past.
  - Never teach a required mechanic with a book alone. Books have a skill and level gate that silently excludes exactly the players who need the lesson.
  - Any sign, rune, or book that describes a monster's resistances, a god, or a recipe gets the matching ''knowledge_marker''. It costs one line and it is the only teaching that persists.
  - Demonstrate before you test. One weakened, isolated, escapable instance of the gimmick, then the real encounter.
  - Set teaching books to ''level 0'' so a level 1 literate character can actually read them.

==== Making it measurable ====

This list has the same problem as the rest of the article — it is all taste until something counts it. The counting is cheap here: teaching objects are archetypes, so a scan for ''magic_mouth'', ''rune_mark'', ''sign'', and ''knowledge_marker'' per map is the same kind of pass [[https://github.com/tannerrj/crossfire-map-audit|Crossfire Map Report]] already makes for monsters and treasure. Cross that count against the map's ''difficulty'' and its monster list and the output is a short list of maps that are hard and say nothing, which is the actual thing worth fixing.

===== Who Crossfire is actually for, expanded =====

The article's point is that trying to serve everybody produces a game that serves nobody, so pick your player and design for them. Crossfire has picked one, just never out loud: somebody who will alt-tab to a wiki, keep notes, tolerate a system that does not explain itself, and treat working out the rules as part of the game. Thirty years of content has been built for that person by people who are that person.

The trap is assuming the only alternative is to dumb the game down. It is not, and conflating the two is what keeps this conversation from going anywhere. Opacity is not depth. A fire elemental being immune to fire is depth. A fire elemental being immune to fire //and the game never saying so anywhere// is opacity, and removing the second changes nothing about the first. Every example below keeps the systems exactly as they are and only changes what the game is willing to tell you about them.

==== Serving the archetype without requiring it ====

The structural answer is layering: information the curious player can pull, rather than a lecture pushed at everybody. That costs the veteran nothing, because a veteran does not read the sign.

**Make the depth opt-in, not mandatory.** An NPC with ''@match'' dialogue is the cleanest version of this in the engine. The player who wants to be told can ask; the player who wants to work it out walks past and never sees the text. The same map serves both, which is the closest anybody gets to beating the article's rule. ''scorn/misc/beginners_entry'' already does this - the ''@question'' and ''@reply'' lines advertise what is askable, so a curious player can pull three layers deep and everyone else can ignore it.

**State the target, not the answer.** A sign at a dungeon entrance reading "the guard captain lost six men in here, all of them better armed than you" tells a level 5 character to come back later without revealing a single mechanic. Danger signalling and mechanic teaching are separate jobs, and the first one is uncontroversial - nobody's sense of discovery is ruined by knowing a dungeon is deadly.

**Two doors into the same content.** Where a dungeon has a punishing route and a slower safe one, say so at the fork and let the player choose their own difficulty. This is player-authored difficulty selection with no engine support required, and it does not fragment the playerbase across separate content the way difficulty settings would.

**Let the game keep the notes.** ''knowledge_marker'' is the in-game replacement for a spoiler file. The player still has to find the book, kill the thing, or ask the right NPC - the discovery loop is intact - but what they discover is recorded in-game instead of in a browser tab. This is the single most important idea in these notes: the goal is not to remove the spoiler file, it is to move it inside the game where it can be earned.

**Gate the consequence, not the information.** A teaching room where the gimmick cannot kill you, followed by the real encounter where it can. The lesson stays discovered rather than told, and the player who already knows loses ten seconds walking through room one.

**Say who the region is for.** Region-level and entrance-level level guidance costs nothing and lets a player self-select. An explicitly flagged "no hand-holding past this point" area is a legitimate design statement, and stating it is what turns the archetype from an accident into a decision.

==== Games in the same position ====

Crossfire is not unusual here, and the useful part is what each of these did about it.

**NetHack.** The archetype, and the reason "spoiler file" is a genre term. In-game there is ''#help'', an oracle selling vague hints, and not much else; the actual manual is a community-maintained pile of text files. Its community treats reading them as part of playing rather than as a failure, which is the same position Crossfire occupies by default. Worth knowing that this is a defensible end state - it just should be chosen.

**Dungeon Crawl Stone Soup.** The instructive one. It forked from Linley's Dungeon Crawl and its design documents treat spoiler-dependence as a bug: content whose correct play required knowing something the game never showed you got reworked or removed, and in-game monster and item descriptions are detailed enough that examining a thing tells you how it fights. Crawl did not get easier. It got legible. That distinction is the whole argument, and Crawl is the closest thing to a proof that a roguelike community can make this decision without losing its identity.

**Dwarf Fortress.** The extreme case for over a decade - enormous simulation, an interface that told you almost nothing, a wiki that was mandatory equipment. The 2022 commercial release added a tutorial and rebuilt the interface without simplifying the simulation underneath it. Depth and opacity came apart cleanly, which is the counterexample to hand anyone who argues that explaining Crossfire would ruin it.

**Caves of Qud.** Built modern with the teaching inside the game: item, mutation, and effect descriptions state their actual mechanics, and the setting is delivered as in-world text rather than as an out-of-game manual. Still deeply strange, still punishing. Roughly the target state for a Crossfire that keeps its character.

**EVE Online.** The closest structural match - persistent, shared economy, no way to scale difficulty per player without breaking fairness, which is the same corner the article's "magic sauce" section puts Crossfire in. Also the cautionary tale: it never solved the out-of-game problem, and third-party tools and spreadsheets became part of the effective client. Its partial answers were tutorial chains and career agents, which is to say teaching content placed inside the world.

**Angband and ADOM** are the near relatives - same lineage, same reliance on external spoiler files, same "you had to know" moments - and **Path of Exile** is the endpoint worth avoiding, where an external build planner is not optional and everyone has quietly agreed that the game ships incomplete.

==== What has to change to move the source in-game ====

The gap is smaller than it looks, because most of the machinery is already built and idle.

**The topical help slot exists and is empty.** ''server/c_misc.cpp'' has three help categories: commands, wiz commands, and //misc//. The misc branch reads ''$datadir/mischelp'' - and that directory does not exist in the tree. ''opendir'' fails, the function returns silently, and ''help topics'' prints an empty "Misc help:" line. The engine has a purpose-built home for exactly the documentation these notes say is missing, and nobody has ever put anything in it.

**And it is not wired up anyway.** ''find_help_file()'' searches ''wizhelp'' and ''help'' only. Drop a file into ''mischelp'' today and it would be //listed// under "Misc help" and still be unreadable, because ''help <topic>'' would not find it. Two more ''find_help_file_in()'' calls fixes that. It is the smallest engine change on this list and it unblocks all of the rest.

**Of 96 help topics, all but about three document the interface.** Most of ''lib/help'' covers keystrokes and syntax: ''apply'', ''bind'', ''pickup'', ''shoottype''. The exceptions are ''death.en'' and ''spells.en'', with ''traps.en'' a partial third, and ''death.en'' in particular is well written - which proves the project can produce this material when somebody sits down to do it. There is still no ''itempower'', no ''paths'', no ''praying'', no ''resistances'', no ''curse'', no ''literacy'', no ''encumbrance'', and no ''alchemy''. A player can learn every keystroke in the game and still not be told that armour weight costs them spell point regeneration. The problem is not that nobody can write these; it is that two got written and the effort stopped.

**The starting help page is eight lines.** ''lib/def_help'' explains how to use ''help'' and how to file a bug. That is the game's front door for a new player, and it points at a topic list that is entirely commands.

**Nobody finds out ''knowledge'' exists.** ''server/knowledge.cpp'' prints a one-time hint about the ''knowledge'' command the first time a player is granted any knowledge at all. With three files in the map set using ''knowledge_marker'', most players are never granted any, so they never see the hint, so the feature is invisible. It is circular, and the map side is what breaks the loop.

**Help text is already clickable.** ''help_topics()'' wraps every entry in ''[help]...[/help]'' markup so clients can render topics as links. The presentation layer is done. This argues for writing the content, since it will not land as a wall of terminal text.

Ordered by cost, the work looks like this:

  - Add the two ''find_help_file_in("mischelp", ...)'' calls so topical help resolves at all.
  - Create ''lib/mischelp'' and convert ''doc/survival-guide.txt'', ''doc/skills.txt'', and ''doc/runes-guide.txt'' into topic files. The text exists; this is editing, not writing.
  - Write the missing mechanic topics - item power, spell paths, praying and gods, resistances, curses, literacy, encumbrance, alchemy, weapon improvement - following the existing ''.en'' and ''.fr'' naming convention.
  - Rewrite ''lib/def_help'' to point a new player at the mechanic topics first and the command list second.
  - Start attaching ''knowledge_marker'' to the signs and books that already describe monsters, gods, and recipes, so the knowledge system stops being invisible.
  - Give new characters one readable in-game item that says the ''help'' and ''knowledge'' commands exist.

None of that removes a single system, changes a single monster, or makes the game easier. It changes the game from one that requires a second screen into one that merely rewards it, and the archetype in line one is still the player it is built for.

===== The death penalty, expanded =====

==== What actually happens when you die ====

The one-line summary above - exp loss plus stat depletion - is right but incomplete, and the incomplete part is where the fixes live. From ''kill_player_not_permadeath()'' in ''server/player.cpp:3599'', in order:

  - One random stat of seven is depleted. With the default ''stat_loss_on_death false'' this is a recoverable ''depletion'' object in your inventory, not permanent stat loss.
  - A gravestone is placed on the square where you died, naming you, your title, and your killer.
  - ''restore_player()'' clears poison, disease, and confusion.
  - ''apply_death_exp_penalty()'' runs, per skill and again on total exp.
  - Food is topped up to 900 if it was under 100, and hp, sp, and grace are refilled.
  - Unpaid shop items are stripped.
  - You are teleported to your last savebed, unbraced, with pickup inhibited, and saved.
  - If a spell effect is sitting on your savebed square, you get a 50-tick immunity force so you cannot be spawn-killed.

Two things stand out. **No items are lost** - your gear comes with you, which is already more merciful than most of the genre. And death partially //heals// you: full hp, sp, grace, food, and every status effect cleared. The engine is not as punitive as its reputation, and saying so makes the criticism of what remains more credible rather than less.

The exp penalty itself, from ''apply_death_exp_penalty()'' in ''common/living.cpp:2244'', is the lesser of three numbers, computed **per skill**: ''death_penalty_percentage'' of that skill's exp, the exp equivalent of ''death_penalty_levels'' levels, or half of your non-permanent exp. Defaults in ''lib/config/settings'' are 20 percent, 3 levels, and ''permanent_experience_percentage 50''. So on a default server you cannot lose more than a quarter of your total exp no matter how badly things go, and half of it can never be lost at all.

The per-skill part is the ugly bit. Every skill in your inventory takes the hit, including the ten you were not using when you died. A character who dies while meleeing loses literacy exp, bargaining exp, and praying exp along with it.

**The depletion is curable and most players do not know how.** ''remove_depletion()'' is reachable three ways: a potion of life, the restoration spell, or praying at your god's altar. Ten of the game's eleven gods grant ''god_remove_depletion'' at ''chance 50'' with no level gate - Gnarg, Lythander, Mostrai, Valkyrie, Gaea, Ixalovh, Ruggilli, Sorig, Gorokh, and Valriel. The only holdout is Devourers, which is at least thematically honest. Praying at your own altar long enough is free and fixes everything.

That reframes the section. The death penalty is not really a difficulty penalty. It is a penalty for not having read the wiki - a player who knows about praying pays nothing but time, and a player who does not stays weakened indefinitely and concludes the game is punishing them. Recoverable depletion that the player cannot discover how to cure is functionally identical to permanent stat loss: same outcome, worse feeling, and fixable with a sign. It is the mechanic surface problem wearing a different hat.

It also helps to separate three things that get discussed as one. At death the numbers go down, you are weakened until you cure it, and you have to walk back. Only the first is the article's "increased difficulty" problem. The second is a teaching problem and the third is a map layout problem. They have different owners and different fixes, and lumping them together is why this conversation never lands.

==== No code changes: server configuration ====

The penalty is already tunable in ''lib/config/settings'' without touching a line of C++. A server admin who thinks death hurts too much can act today:

  * ''permanent_experience_percentage'' (default 50) - raise it and less exp is ever at risk. At 100 the exp penalty disappears entirely.
  * ''death_penalty_percentage'' (default 20) and ''death_penalty_levels'' (default 3) - the two caps on a single death's exp loss. Lower either.
  * ''stat_loss_on_death'' (default false) - already set to the merciful option, giving recoverable depletion rather than permanent stat loss. Worth knowing it exists, because true is much harsher.
  * ''balanced_stat_loss'' (default false) - scales stat loss by level, gentler on low-level characters and harsher on high-level ones. Arguably the better setting for a game that wants new players to survive their first week, and the article's argument supports turning it on.

NOTE: none of this is a map or code change. A public test server could run softened values for a season and compare retention, which is the monitoring point applied to the one variable that is already adjustable.

==== No code changes: content and maps ====

**Teach the cure at the point of pain.** A depleted player is standing at their savebed, which is in an inn or an apartment, which is a map somebody owns. A sign there naming the three cures - potion of life, restoration, praying at your god's altar - reaches every player at the exact moment the information is worth something. ''start/newbieshouse'' already has this text on a sign; it exists in precisely one place in the game.

**Put the potions where the deaths are.** Potions of life are tiered by level: minor for under 5 (value 500), medium for under 30 (3000), major for under 50 (10000), supreme for under 130 (20000). A level 4 character who dies needs a 500-value potion, which is affordable, and has no idea it exists. Stocking them visibly in temple and healer shops near starting content, with a sign, costs nothing.

**Say it in the temples.** Every temple map with an altar is a place where the cure is free. An NPC or sign in each of the ten temples whose god grants restoration, stating plainly that praying here long enough will restore stats lost to death, would close most of the gap on its own. It also gives the temples a reason to exist for non-priests.

**Shorten the walk.** 155 maps in the set contain a ''bed_save'', concentrated in cities. The pain of death for an experienced player is not the exp, which regenerates, it is the trip back. Inns or camps with savebeds near major dungeon clusters are a pure map-side fix and need no permission from anybody.

**Use the gravestone.** It is already placed on the exact square where the player died, with the killer's name on it. Map authors can lean on that as a landmark - a returning player can find where they died. It is also, incidentally, the closest thing Crossfire already has to death-location tracking, just rendered as an object instead of a log line.

==== No code changes: data files ====

''lib/help/death.en'' already exists and is genuinely good - it explains savebed transport, exp loss, depletion, the potion tiers, where to buy them (House of Healing in Scorn), and praying at your cult's altar. Somebody wrote the right document years ago.

The problem is discovery. It sits in ''lib/help'' among 96 files that are otherwise almost entirely command references, and reaching it requires typing ''help death'', which requires already suspecting that a topic named "death" exists. Fixes that are text edits, not code:

  * Point at it from ''lib/def_help'', which is currently eight lines about how to use ''help'' and how to file a bug.
  * Reference it in the ''motd'' and in the ''rules'' file.
  * Put its content in an in-game book in the starting area, and on the sign by the savebed.
  * Add ''knowledge_marker'' to that sign so the lesson persists in the player's ''knowledge'' list.

==== Code changes ====

Ordered roughly by cost. The first two are almost free and worth doing regardless of what else gets decided.

=== Tell the player what just happened ===

The death sequence ends with ''draw_ext_info(... "YOU HAVE DIED.")'' and nothing else. The player is not told how much exp they lost, which skills lost it, which stat was depleted, or that the depletion is curable. Every one of those numbers is already computed a few lines earlier.

**Change:** have ''apply_death_exp_penalty()'' return a summary and print it with the death message - total exp lost, the worst-hit skill, the depleted stat by name, and a one-line pointer to ''help death''. Roughly 30 lines across ''common/living.cpp'' and ''server/player.cpp''. **Risk:** none, it is pure output with no rules change.

This is the highest value-to-effort item on the list. Most of the perceived cruelty of Crossfire death is that it happens silently. Going further, a death message that named the attacktype that finished you would teach most of the resistance system over a few characters.

=== Make the gravestone do what its comment says ===

''server/player.cpp:3726'' opens with "Put a gravestone up where the character 'almost' died. **List the exp loss on the stone.**" The code below it writes name, title, and killer, and no exp loss. The comment has been lying for years.

**Change:** add the loss figures to the gravestone ''msg''. Ten lines, and it makes the death location self-documenting for the player who walks back. **Risk:** none.

=== Log deaths in a machine-readable form ===

The enabling change for the death-location aggregation further down this page, and it belongs here because ''kill_player()'' is where the data is.

**Change:** one log line per death with map path, x, y, player level, killer name, and exp lost - either a dedicated file or a plugin hook, perhaps 20 lines. **Risk:** disk growth and privacy on a public server; log rotation and player-name hashing handle both.

Everything the monitoring section wants falls out of this, and it makes every other proposal here testable instead of arguable.

=== Stop taxing skills the player was not using ===

The per-skill loop in ''apply_death_exp_penalty()'' hits every skill with exp. Dying to a monster should not cost you bargaining progress.

**Change:** a new setting, ''death_penalty_skills'', with values ''all'' (current behaviour, the default) or ''used'', where ''used'' restricts the penalty to the skill applied at time of death plus the total-exp hit. **Risk:** low, and gated behind a setting that defaults to today's behaviour. Needs a decision on which skill counts when a player dies to a trap or to poison with no skill applied - falling back to the total-exp hit alone is fine.

=== A floor for low-level characters ===

''balanced_stat_loss'' already recognises that low-level characters suffer disproportionately, but only for stats. Exp has no such protection, and a level 3 character losing 20 percent of a small number still loses a meaningful fraction of their week.

**Change:** ''death_penalty_min_level'', default 0 to preserve current behaviour, below which no exp is lost and depletion is skipped. Three lines in ''apply_death_exp_penalty()'' and a guard in the stat block. **Risk:** exploitable only by staying low level, which is self-limiting.

=== Soul recovery at the gravestone ===

The classic answer to this problem in the wider genre, and Crossfire is one object away from it. The gravestone already exists, already sits on the square where you died, and is already named after you.

**Change:** stash the lost exp on the gravestone at creation. A player who returns and applies their own gravestone recovers some fraction of it - half is the conventional number - and the stone crumbles. Non-owners get nothing, and expiry is automatic because the stone goes with the map reset.

This converts the penalty from a tax into content. The player who died gets a reason to go back in with the same monsters still there, which is exactly the backward callback the article likes, and it self-balances: recovering the exp means surviving the thing that killed you.

**Risk:** the real one. In a shared-economy game, exp recovery has to be owner-locked or it becomes a trade good, and a player who dies repeatedly in one room should not stack ten stones. Cap it at one recoverable stone per player, replace on new death, and make the fraction a setting. **Effort:** moderate - a new apply handler for the gravestone plus owner tracking. This is the one to prototype on a test server rather than commit to.

=== Depletion decay ===

**Change:** an optional setting that removes one point of depletion per N hours of play time, so a player who never learns about praying is not permanently weakened.

**Risk:** it undercuts the potion economy and the temples, which is a real cost. Better to fix the teaching than route around it, and this is listed mainly to argue against it - if the information problem is solved, depletion stops being a trap and this becomes unnecessary.

=== Make help text reflect the running server ===

''death.en'' says loss amounts "vary by server configuration" because it cannot know them. The server does.

**Change:** allow simple substitution tokens in help files so the topic can state the actual ''death_penalty_percentage'' this server runs. Small, and it applies to more than this one file.

==== Before tuning anything ====

Every number in the configuration section is a knob somebody can turn on opinion. The death log is 20 lines and it is what turns this into evidence - same conclusion as everywhere else on this page.

And watch the multiplayer angle. Any change here interacts with the shared economy: soul recovery is tradeable if you are careless, depletion decay devalues potions of life, and a lighter penalty changes the risk calculus for high-level players farming low-level content. None of these is a blocker, but each needs the party and economy case thought through before it ships.

===== Hidden mechanics as puzzle solutions, expanded =====

==== Puzzle or lockout ====

There is a clean test for this and it is worth stating before the examples. A **puzzle** asks the player to combine things the game has shown them. A **lockout** asks them to already know something the game never showed them anywhere. Both feel identical from the author's chair, because the author knows the answer. They are completely different from the player's side: one is solvable by thinking harder, the other is solvable only by alt-tabbing.

The test is a single question - //where in the game could a player have learned this?// If the honest answer is "another map, and nothing points there" or "the wiki" or "you would have to try it", it is a lockout. Note that the answer does not have to be on the same map. A rumour in a tavern three towns over is a legitimate answer, as long as something connects the two.

==== How much of this is there ====

The gating mechanisms are ''check_inv'', ''altar_trigger'', and ''detector'', all keyed on a ''slaying'' string naming the required item. Across the map set, excluding editor, test, template, and unlinked directories:

  * **464 maps** contain at least one item-keyed gate.
  * **5,689 item-keyed trigger altars** across **225 maps** - the "put the right thing on the right square" mechanism.
  * **453 maps** contain a ''pedestal'', the same idea driven by weight rather than by item identity.
  * **389 of those trigger altars are ''invisible 1''**, across **12 maps**. The mechanism itself cannot be seen.

NOTE: the counts are the easy part. The hard part is that a scan can tell you a gate exists and cannot tell you whether the map explains it, which is the next section.

==== Why the naive audit does not work ====

The obvious measurement is "gated maps containing no sign, book, rune, mouth, or NPC dialogue," and it comes out at about 16 percent of gated maps. That number is too flattering, and the reasons are instructive for anybody building the tooling:

  * ''bookshelf'' matches a search for books and carries no text at all. Two of them sit in the worst example below and made it score as a map with teaching objects.
  * ''scorn/temples/gorokh_final'' contains a sign. It reads "Dungeon Master's Lounge."
  * Guild maps are full of gates keyed on ''Stove'', ''Forge'', ''Cauldron'', and ''TanningDesk'' - crafting furniture, explained by the act of joining a guild, not really puzzles at all.

A useful audit has to check three things, not one: that a teaching object exists, that it is **near** the gate, and that its text actually **names the requirement**. The third is the only one that matters and the only one nobody has scanned for.

==== The bad case ====

''mlab/citydeclouds/cwdcguestofmarquis1'' - "Guest of the Marquis", 64x63, ''difficulty 45''.

Five unremarkable squares near the middle of the map carry **45 stacked trigger altars**, every one of them ''invisible 1'', each named after a creature and keyed to a different item:

<code>
arch altar_trigger
name balrog
slaying demon_head
food 1
x 47
y 26
invisible 1
connected 66685
end
</code>

The full demand list across those five squares runs to gold nuggets, sulphur, ruby, bat wing, four different colours of rose, a demon's head, mint, salt, an apple, a mace, and a rock. The map contains no sign, no ''magic_mouth'', no ''rune_mark'', no NPC ''@match'' dialogue, and no readable book - the only two book-shaped objects are scenery bookshelves with no message. Nothing anywhere on the map indicates that those squares are different from any other square, let alone which of a dozen items each one wants.

This is the article's "don't" in its purest available form. The mechanism is invisible, the requirement is unguessable, and there is no in-game path to the answer. A player either has the wiki open or they do not finish the map.

==== The good case ====

''wolfsburg/shops/transport.shoppe'' uses **the same hidden mechanism** - 11 invisible item-keyed altars taking payment - and is completely legible, because it also carries a numbered sign, two magic mouths, and NPC dialogue:

<code>
Information on using our teleporters
------------------------------
1. Supply the proper payment.
2. Step forward *quickly*.
3. Apply the teleporter or wait a moment for the teleporter to activate.
</code>

Same engine feature, opposite outcome. This is the whole argument in two maps: invisible altars are not the problem, unexplained invisible altars are. Nothing about the mechanism needed to change.

==== The middle case ====

''scorn/temples/gorokh_final'' has an altar keyed to ''apple'', and an apple placed somewhere on the map. Four magic mouths deliver atmosphere - "Only the Advocate may enter", "Gorokh has noticed you" - and not one of them mentions the apple or the altar.

This one is nearly fine. The required item is physically present, so a thorough player can solve it by experiment, which makes it a puzzle rather than a lockout. But the map already has four mouths, and one more sentence in any of them would turn "I wandered around picking things up" into "I worked out what the temple wanted." The cost of the fix is a line of text in a file that already exists.

==== The common shapes and what to do about each ====

**The unmarked trigger square.** Invisible ''altar_trigger'' or ''pedestal'' with no visual difference from its neighbours. Fix: make it visible, or leave it invisible and mark the floor around it - a rune, a mosaic, scorch marks, a body slumped against the wall holding the right item. The mechanism can stay hidden as long as the //location// is not.

**The unguessable ingredient.** A gate keyed to a specific item with nothing naming it. Fix: name it somewhere reachable. A previous petitioner's journal, an NPC who complains about the price of roses, a mouth that says what the last person tried and how it went. Attach ''knowledge_marker'' so the answer survives the session.

**The off-map answer.** The solution is written down, in a different map, with nothing connecting them. This is the most defensible of the four and still needs a thread - a rumour, a torn page, an NPC who names the town.

**The untaught engine mechanic.** The puzzle needs marking runes, pushing objects, a specific spell, or the ''search'' skill, and the game never taught any of them. Fix: this is not a puzzle problem, it is the mechanic surface problem. Teach the mechanic somewhere survivable first, then a puzzle built on it is fair.

==== Conventions worth adding to Info/mapguide ====

  - Every gate keyed on ''slaying'' needs an in-game answer somewhere, and the author should be able to name the map and object that provides it.
  - If the trigger is ''invisible 1'', something visible must mark the square. Hide the mechanism, not the location.
  - Atmosphere text is not teaching text. A map can have five magic mouths and still tell the player nothing, and ''gorokh_final'' is the proof.
  - Scenery does not count. A ''bookshelf'' is furniture; only a readable with a ''msg'' teaches anything.
  - Where the answer lives on another map, put a thread between them.

The audit that would find the remaining cases is not the one already built - it needs proximity and text matching, not just object counts. But it is the same scan, and the 12 maps with invisible item-keyed altars are a short enough list to review by hand this afternoon.

===== Adrenaline pacing and backward callbacks, expanded =====

These get named together in the source article and they are not the same job. **Adrenaline pacing** is intensity over time inside a single run - the rise and fall that makes the peaks feel like peaks. **Backward callbacks** are content that reaches back to something the player already met and re-frames it with what they have since gained. One is map layout, the other is the quest system, and they have different owners in this project.

==== Crossfire opens at full intensity, and it is measurable ====

The claim at the top of these notes - that most dungeons open at full intensity on room one - turns out to be checkable. Taking every non-overworld map with at least one hostile monster and measuring the distance from the map's declared entry point to the nearest one:

  * **1,062 maps** qualify, excluding the world tiles, editor, test, template, and unlinked directories.
  * The **median distance to the first hostile is 5 tiles**.
  * 35 percent have one within 3 tiles, 59 percent within 5, 79 percent within 8, and 90 percent within 12.
  * Median dungeon or town map area is 484 tiles, roughly 22x22, and 45 percent are 20x20 or smaller.
  * Median monster density is 1.9 per 100 tiles, which is not high - the monsters are not crowded, they are just //immediately// present.

Five tiles is inside the player's view on arrival. There is no vestibule, no approach, no beat where the player reads the room. The map starts and the fight starts.

NOTE: the measurement is Chebyshev distance and knows nothing about walls, so a monster three tiles away behind a wall counts the same as one in the corridor. It also counts a monster as hostile unless its archetype or its map instance sets ''friendly'' or ''unaggressive'', which will still misfile a few caged or scripted NPCs. The numbers are a floor for the effect, not a precise census - but the direction is not in doubt, and the same scan run per region would show which map sets are worst.

==== What pacing looks like in map terms ====

The article's saw shape needs somewhere to dip, and a 20x20 map with monsters five tiles from the door has nowhere. Concrete devices, all map-side:

**The vestibule.** Three to five tiles of nothing between the entrance and the first threat. It costs almost no space and it is the single cheapest change on this list. The player arrives, sees the room, and chooses to advance - which turns the first fight from an ambush into a decision.

**The breather room.** A lit, monster-free room in the middle of the dungeon. No treasure required, no puzzle, nothing to do but stand still and let spell points come back. The absence of threat is the content. NOTE: keep wandering monsters and generators out of it, or it is not a breather.

**Modulated density rather than an even sprinkle.** A median of 1.9 monsters per 100 tiles spread evenly is a flat line. The same monster budget clustered into three pockets with quiet corridors between them is a saw. Same monsters, same exp, different experience.

**Tension without damage.** Darkness, sound, a locked door that rattles, a corridor of gravestones, a boss visible through bars long before the player can reach it. All of these raise intensity without spending hit points, which is exactly what the low part of the saw is for.

**Decompression after the peak.** Do not put the exit one tile past the boss. A short walk back through the now-empty dungeon is where the win registers, and it is also where the player counts their loot instead of their potions.

==== Backward callbacks: what the quest system already supports ====

The ''.quests'' files are better machinery than their usage suggests. A quest definition carries ''title'', ''face'', ''description'', an optional ''comment'' that players never see, ''restart'', and a list of numbered steps, one of which is flagged ''finishes_quest''. Two features matter here:

**''parent''** makes a quest a child of another, so a chain can be modelled as a tree rather than a line.

**''setwhen''** attaches conditions to a step, so it advances automatically when //other// quests reach given states. From ''include/quest.h'' the condition grammar is ''questcode 20'' for an exact step, ''questcode <=20'' for at most, ''questcode 10-20'' for a range, and ''questcode finished''.

That is precisely the mechanism a callback needs: "this step opens because you finished that other thing." The best worked example in the set is ''darcap/darcap/elemental.quests'', where a parent quest waits on four children:

<code>
step 20
description
I have solved the four elemental quests, I can now claim my reward.
end_description
setwhen
darcap/Elemental 10
darcap/Elemental-Fire 20
darcap/Elemental-Water 20
darcap/Elemental-Air 20
darcap/Elemental-Earth 20
end_setwhen
end_step
</code>

Now the usage. Across 21 ''.quests'' files there are **66 quests and 333 steps**, a median of 5 steps each, with ''scorn/CoffeeGoose'' the longest at 12. But there are only **24 ''setwhen'' blocks and 15 ''parent'' declarations, spread across 4 of the 21 files**. The cross-quest conditional - the one feature that makes a chain a chain rather than a list - is used by well under a tenth of the content.

**The constraint worth knowing before planning around this.** Quest state is advanced from Python, through ''cf_quest_start'' and ''cf_quest_set_player_state''; there is no map object that reads or writes it. So a callback where the //map itself// changes based on quest state needs a script, not just archetypes. Worse, ''python/CFDialog.py'' keeps its own per-player semaphore state that is separate from the quest system, so a lot of existing dialogue-driven progression is invisible to ''.quests'' entirely. Anybody building on this should decide which of the two state systems they are using before writing the first step.

==== Callback patterns worth building ====

**The door you already walked past.** Put a locked or impassable feature early, in plain sight, and open it at a later quest step. The player has already built a memory of the place, so the return costs no explanation. This needs only a ''setwhen'' and something to flip the gate.

**The monster that beat you, met again.** The article's version of this is re-using an obstacle once the player has the tool for it. In Crossfire terms: the fire immune thing in the level 5 dungeon, met again at level 20 in a wing that was sealed the first time. It also completes the teaching started in the earlier map, which ties this to the mechanic surface section - a mechanic taught once and re-tested later reads as a subject rather than a gimmick.

**The NPC who remembers.** ''@match'' dialogue keyed on quest state, so the guard who turned you away at step 10 greets you by name at step 40. Cheap, and it does more for the sense of a persistent world than most map work.

**The quest that reframes the first dungeon.** The strongest version, and the one nothing in the set currently does: a late quest whose steps send the player back through starting-area maps for a reason that was not visible the first time. It costs no new maps, which is the entire appeal.

==== Conventions worth adding to Info/mapguide ====

  - Leave three to five tiles between the entry point and the first hostile. If the map is too small for that, it is too small to open a dungeon.
  - Every dungeon of more than two levels gets at least one monster-free, generator-free room.
  - Spend the monster budget in clusters with quiet between them, not spread evenly across the floor.
  - Do not place the exit adjacent to the final encounter.
  - When building a chain, use ''parent'' and ''setwhen'' rather than a flat list of steps, and pick one state system - quest steps or dialogue semaphores - and stay in it.

The entry-distance scan is about thirty lines and runs over the whole map set in seconds. Run per region, it produces a ranked list of the maps that open worst, which is a concrete work queue rather than an opinion about pacing.

===== What does not apply cleanly =====

**Dynamic difficulty adjustment ("magic sauce").** In a persistent multiplayer game with a shared economy, scaling difficulty to the individual breaks exp and loot fairness and is trivially exploitable — deliberately underperform, get an easier run at the same reward. Crossfire already has the defensible version of this: random maps that scale by difficulty parameter, and party/level-scaled content.

**"Never allow skipping unskilled sections."** Crossfire is an open world on purpose. You cannot enforce sequence, and trying to would be a worse game. The article's advice here is platformer-specific.

**Formal QA, expo testing, review sites.** No budget, no publisher, no funnel. The analog is a public test server plus new-player feedback threads.

===== What Crossfire should actually adopt =====

  - **Audit the ''difficulty'' header against map contents.** Roughly 1,120 of ~1,220 maps in the city regions set ''difficulty'' by hand, with no cross-author consistency, and the field drives treasure generation (the server computes it from monster experience only when it is unset). A report comparing each map's declared difficulty against its actual monster exp/level content, flagging outliers, is the single highest-value change here — and [[https://github.com/tannerrj/crossfire-map-audit|Crossfire Map Report]] already does the scanning half of that job.
  - **Aggregate death locations.** The server logs player deaths; rolling those up per map and per coordinate gives you the article's evenly-distributed-deaths test with real data instead of opinion. This turns "that dungeon feels unfair" into a heatmap. Everything else on this list gets easier once it exists.
  - **Signal danger before the door.** Crossfire dungeon entrances are anonymous holes in the ground. The article's "subtle graphical hints" translate to sign objects, entrance-adjacent corpses and wreckage, and map ''msg'' blocks — cheap, purely map-side, no engine work. [[https://github.com/tannerrj/crossfire-world-map-entrances|Crossfire World Map Entrances]] already enumerates entrances, so the coverage gap is measurable.
  - **Teach at the point of need, using the modalities that already exist.** Signs, marking runes, magic mouths, books, NPC ''@match'' dialogue, and ''knowledge_marker'' are all in the engine today and underused (map ''msg'' and region ''msg'' only surface on ''mapinfo'' and ''whereami'', so they are flavour rather than teaching). A monster with an immunity that matters should have a corpse, a book, or a rambling NPC nearby that says so.
  - **Give each dungeon a teaching room.** If a map's gimmick is spell-reflecting monsters, invisibility, or a timed gate, room one should demonstrate it somewhere survivable. This is a map-authoring convention, not code — worth writing into ''Info/mapguide''.
  - **Keep doing what the recent altar work does.** The last several commits added second altars to temples for multi-player friendliness. That is the article's "player diversity" principle applied without being told to — reducing contention so different player populations can use the same content. The same instinct should extend to chokepoints in quest maps.

The two to start with are the difficulty audit and death-location aggregation, because both are tooling that is already 60% built, and both convert the rest of the list from taste into evidence.
